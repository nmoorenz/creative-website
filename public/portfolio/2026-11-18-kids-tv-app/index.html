Kids TV App

Built with AI-assisted development: the design, code, and infrastructure were worked out in conversation with an AI assistant, which wrote most of the code and talked through the trade-offs, with a human directing the decisions and reviewing every change.

Kids TV is an Android TV app that lets a young child watch a short list of chosen YouTube channels and nothing else. It takes the thing you would not hand a small child, YouTube, and strips it back to the parts you trust: a grid of a few channels, each show playing inside the app, with no ads, no search, no recommendations, and no way to wander off.

This is a writeup of what it is, the choices behind it, and how it’s built.

The app

The app should run on any Android TV, but needs to be downloaded from GitHub and sideloaded to be installed. The home screen is a row of channels. Move around the channels and the episodes in each channel, pick a card and the episode plays. That is the whole interface. There is no search box, no up-next rail, no comments, and nothing autoplaying a stranger’s video after the credits.

The channels

The channels are chosen by hand: Numberblocks, Kid Crew, David Rule, Half-Asleep Chris, Octonauts, Postman Pat. Where a show has a real season and episode structure the app keeps it, but otherwise there is just an episode list. The hour-long “all episodes back to back” compilations are left out, and what stays is individual episodes at a sensible length.

What is on the TV is just a list the app reads. There is no regular updates to the app, unless YouTube changes something about fetching videos.

Staying current

Some shows are static like Octonauts and Postman Pat. Some shows still release regularly. A scheduled job runs each night, re-checks the chosen channels, and include any new episodes into the metadata, so the latest episode of a show turns up on the TV on its own, usually by the next morning. Choosing or dropping a whole channel is a deliberate change; new episodes of the channels already there arrive by themselves.

Playback

Playback was the hard part. You cannot simply embed a YouTube video and expect it to play cleanly, least of all on old hardware: embedding is restricted, and the television’s browser is too old to cope. The first attempt put a web player in a WebView and hit both walls at once, embedding blocked on some videos and the TV rejecting modern security certificates on others.

The version that works embeds nothing. The app pulls the actual video stream on the device and hands it to a native player at 720p. That is what makes it smooth and genuinely ad-free on a 2016 television: there is no web page and no one else’s player, just the video.

Keeping up with YouTube

The part that is never finished is extraction. Pulling a playable stream out of YouTube relies on a community library, the one behind NewPipe and LibreTube, and YouTube changes how it serves video often enough to break it from time to time.

Rather than chase YouTube directly, the app rides on LibreTube. LibreTube is an open-source YouTube app that fights this out full time and keeps a working version of the extraction library, so when playback breaks the repair is to match whatever LibreTube is now using.

Two wrinkles are worth noting. YouTube flags some videos as “made for kids”, and those cannot be extracted this way at all, which is a quiet irony: a few of the most obviously child-friendly toy channels are the ones that will not play. And some channels now carry auto-translated audio, so the app has to choose the original track on purpose, or an episode will suddenly play in Spanish.

In short

Kids TV is a short list of channels, played on a plain screen on an old television, with everything else taken away. The child gets chosen channels, and no path to anything else.
